# Reflection draft — for Bergen to review and personalize

The first version of First Weather established a useful two-day structure, but its strongest promise lived in the concept art rather than in the play experience. The feedback supplied during revision described an interesting world that did not yet feel immersive, appreciation for the new title’s art direction, and uncertainty about why a player would keep going. Those comments became the priorities for this iteration. They are retrospective feedback themes; the three formal playtest sessions still need to be documented.

The most important design change was connecting the story to the systems it asks the player to notice. Morning routines now affect stability, sensory prose and later Pressure. Yesterday’s study and rest change the second class, and a severe episode has a timetable consequence instead of appearing identically on every route. A loving call home can still move the needle. The aim is to let the player notice a contradiction before the narration explains it. The garden similarly asks what to leave short, with different mentor priorities, rather than asking the player to copy a visible numerical answer.

Lola now arrives with her own work, obligations and irritation. Her care for Molly does not remove those needs. The final image is a message about returning together, which gives the next day a personal reason to exist. Whether that invitation is sufficiently compelling remains a question for human players, not a conclusion established by code checks. The dialogue has been moved into an editable Ink file so I can replace the working draft with my own voice while retaining the mechanical responses.

The visual iteration also exposed a practical lesson: matching individual assets does not guarantee a coherent scene. Repainted architecture still looked miniature when combined with an oversized courtyard. The solution was to correct the complete perspective, then keep students, clock, lighting and water as separate moving elements. Painted interface surfaces and typography now draw from the title’s palette, but their success must still be judged at normal playing size and across comfort settings.

Automated checks are useful for complete routes, persistence and text fit. They found a real dialogue-tag problem before review. They cannot tell me whether a line sounds like Molly, whether a relationship feels reciprocal, or whether a room feels inhabited. My next step is therefore three observed playtests with the approved revision, followed by recorded changes and retests. Only after that creative review will I package the prototype and record the pitch. The scope stays two days: enough to demonstrate the game’s central tensions without pretending the full quarter or its later revelations already exist.
